Does more data always make a better model?

One question, every week: when does scaling training data improve deep learning—and when do quality, repetition, compute, or infrastructure change the answer?

The hidden cost of “just add more data”

More examples can improve a model, but the same number of tokens can deliver very different value depending on what they contain, how often they repeat, and how much compute you can spend.

“Add more data” is attractive advice because dataset size is easy to count. But size alone says little about how much useful, new information a training run receives. A recent large-scale study of language-model pretraining makes that gap concrete: data preparation can change the scaling curve itself.

Filtering changes the training equation

At ICLR 2026, Ardalani and colleagues studied 23 curated datasets and trained more than 2,000 models, ranging from 100 million to 8 billion parameters and 100 million to 200 billion tokens. They compared interventions including deduplication, heuristic filtering, and model-guided rewriting. Those choices shifted both the coefficients and exponents of fitted scaling laws. The compute-optimal balance between tokens and parameters varied by orders of magnitude across interventions. In the authors’ words, “data quality ranking is scale and resource-dependent.”

That finding does not mean “filter everything as aggressively as possible.” Filtering changes the supply of examples available for training. If the remaining pool is small, a long run may cycle through the same material again and again.

Cleaner data can still be overused

An ICML 2026 study, InfoLaw, models how data quality, mixture weights, and repetition affect information gained during pretraining. It reports that in data-limited, overtrained settings, strongly upweighting high-quality data can increase repetition enough to degrade performance. The authors summarize one mechanism as “repetition induces scale-dependent diminishing returns.” The lesson is a balance: low-quality data may waste compute, while repeatedly reusing a narrow high-quality pool can also lose value.

Choose the data mix for the run you can afford

So the useful question is not simply “How large can the dataset get?” It is “At this compute budget, which mixture gives the best measured improvement on the task I care about?” That answer can change with model size, training duration, domain, and how much genuinely new data is available. The studies above concern language-model pretraining; treat their specific scaling results as evidence for that setting, not a universal law for every deep-learning task.

Bulleted breakdown

  • Count new information, not only tokens. Deduplication and source diversity help reveal whether added volume brings new coverage.
  • Track repeats. A small clean dataset can become less useful when the training run reuses it too often.
  • Compare under the same budget. Evaluate curated and larger mixtures with matched model, compute, and held-out tests.
  • Measure the marginal gain. Record validation improvement alongside the cost of sourcing, filtering, storing, and processing the extra data.
  • Make the choice task-specific. A dataset mix that helps one capability or domain may not be best for another.

The takeaway: Scaling data can help, but “more” is not a strategy by itself. Treat data quality, repetition, and compute as parts of one experiment—and keep the mixture that earns its cost on the evaluation that matters.

After review · practical direction

Make the data mix measurable and revisable

As a practical inference from this week’s evidence, compare data mixtures on held-out evaluations and record the marginal gain per added unit of data and processing cost.

Why this matters: A decision log lets the next training run build on measured results instead of repeating a scale-first assumption.

Today’s feature · hidden cost

When the training data is the expensive part

For scientific models trained on simulations, each example has a generation cost. A new study treats fidelity—not just dataset size—as a scaling choice.

“Add more data” often assumes that training examples are cheap to collect. That is true for some web corpora; it is a poor assumption for neural surrogates that learn from numerical simulations. Generating a single high-accuracy simulation can consume substantial compute before training even starts. In that setting, the hidden cost of scaling is not only storing more examples—it is producing them.

Make fidelity part of the data budget

A NeurIPS 2026 paper, Compute-optimal data scaling for neural surrogates via multi-fidelity training, studies partial differential equation (PDE) surrogates. The authors treat data quantity and simulation fidelity as joint choices under a fixed data-generation compute budget. A coarse simulation is cheaper but less accurate; a fine simulation costs more. Instead of training only on the expensive end, their approach mixes several fidelity levels.

Across six PDE problems, four surrogate architectures, and more than 7,000 training runs, the authors report that an optimized fidelity mixture can match high-fidelity-only accuracy with up to 6.3 times less data-generation compute. Their experiments cover structured grids and unstructured meshes, as well as discretization and iterative-solver error. The paper describes the bottleneck directly: “the cost of data generation often outweighs the training cost.”

This is not evidence that low-accuracy data is automatically useful. The low- and high-fidelity samples must relate in a way the model can learn from, and accuracy still needs to be checked against held-out high-fidelity simulations. The reported 6.3× is a best result across the paper’s settings, not a guaranteed saving for every simulator.

What changes in the scaling question?

For simulation-driven learning, “how many samples?” is incomplete. A better question is: given the full budget, how should we spend it across sample count and fidelity? Spending everything on a few costly, precise runs can leave useful coverage unexplored. Spending everything on cheap, noisy runs may teach the wrong relationship. A mixture can give the model broad signal cheaply, then preserve more expensive data for calibration and evaluation.

That shift matters for engineering teams because the data pipeline can dominate the training job. Tracking GPU-hours for training alone misses the cost of solver runs, mesh resolution, storage, and validation. As a practical inference, report error against total data-generation compute and keep a high-fidelity holdout set to ensure cheaper examples have not hidden a quality regression.

Bulleted breakdown

  • Count generation cost. Include simulation compute, storage, and validation—not only model-training FLOPs.
  • Record fidelity levels. Track resolution or solver accuracy for every data source.
  • Compare mixtures at matched budgets. Test low-, high-, and mixed-fidelity training under equal data-generation compute.
  • Keep a high-fidelity holdout. Measure whether cheaper data transfers to the accuracy target you actually need.
  • Limit the claim to the evidence. These results are for studied PDE problems and surrogate architectures, not a general rule for all training datasets.

The takeaway: Data volume is not the only scaling lever when examples are costly to generate. For simulation-based models, optimizing the fidelity mix may buy more useful learning per unit of compute than generating only the most precise data.

After review · limits and lessons

“Up to 6.3×” is a setting-specific ceiling

The paper reports savings across six PDE problems, but the amount depends on the task, architecture, and fidelity mix. Treat its headline result as a reason to run a budget-matched pilot, not as a universal forecast.

Why this matters: Verify the error-cost curve for your own data generator before moving budget away from high-fidelity runs.

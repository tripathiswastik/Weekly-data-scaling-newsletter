Today’s feature · myth buster

Filtering a skill may delay it, not erase it

A new study asks whether broad training progress can partly stand in for skill-specific examples. Its answer complicates the idea that removing data reliably removes a capability.

“Use more data” sounds simple: more examples should produce more capability. But a fresh preprint points to a less obvious effect. A model may acquire useful structure for a skill from other parts of its training, even when the skill’s own prediction targets are withheld. Later, it may need surprisingly little direct data to recover that skill.

A controlled way to hide training signal

In General progress substitutes for specific training data in language models, Mark Marosi and Peter Antal use “target thinning”: examples remain in the input stream, but the loss signal for selected skill-specific next-token targets is kept only at a chosen fraction. They test chess, Othello, molecular strings, bracket languages, and web text across models from small experiments to billions of parameters.

This is not simply removing every mention of a subject. The model can still see surrounding context; what is selectively reduced is direct supervision at the skill’s targets. That lets the authors measure how much related structure is learned while the model makes general progress on the rest of the data.

General progress can act like a substitute

In one chess result, a model never explicitly trained on positions where its king was in check ranked legal responses almost as well as a fully supervised comparison. When researchers later returned the withheld targets, the model acquired the measured skill using only a small fraction of the direct data needed from initialization. That fraction fell as the model progressed. A control rule of similar difficulty did not show the same shortcut, suggesting the effect was tied to the skill rather than difficulty alone.

The authors also report a 7-billion-parameter example: after Rust documents were filtered down to 3% of their share, the model closed half its Rust term-accuracy gap using 2.1% of the tokens the unfiltered comparison needed from initialization. This is a concrete warning against treating a data filter as a permanent capability boundary.

What the result does—and does not—say

Evidence: In these experiments, broad training progress supplied information that partly substituted for direct skill targets, and later skill acquisition cost less data. The pattern appears across several constructed domains, with one reported Rust result at 7B scale.

Inference: Filtering may be a weaker control than teams expect if adjacent data teaches reusable structure. Teams should test the capability directly after training and again after continued pretraining or fine-tuning. This is a practical implication, not proof that every filter fails.

Limits: This is a new preprint. Its controlled tasks, metrics, recipes, and selected corpora do not cover every model or capability. A result on chess ranking or Rust term accuracy does not establish what happens for every behavior, nor does it show every additional token improves performance.

Bulleted breakdown

  • Filtering is not always deletion. In target thinning, inputs stay in context while selected loss targets are withheld.
  • Related data can carry signal. Models learned parts of tested skills while training on the rest of the corpus.
  • Recovery can be cheap. Returning direct targets took a small fraction of the from-scratch data in the reported experiments.
  • Scale changes the risk. The paper reports the effect in a 7B Rust example as well as smaller tasks.
  • Validate the exact capability. Treat this as a reason to test after training, not a universal forecast.

The takeaway: More broad training can sometimes make withheld skills easier to acquire, because related structure transfers. Dataset size alone cannot tell whether a capability was removed, preserved indirectly, or made cheaper to relearn.

More signals from today’s research

01 / HIDDEN ASSUMPTION

Removing targets is not removing context

The study leaves skill-bearing inputs in the stream and withholds selected next-token loss targets. The remaining corpus can still teach structure that helps the model perform the skill.

Why this matters: A filter’s effect depends on information around examples, not only the count of removed labels.

After review · what to validate

Re-test after general training, not only after filtering

A capability may be weak immediately after filtering yet easier to recover after the model improves elsewhere. Track held-out evaluations across continued training, and document which training signals were actually removed.

Why this matters: One checkpoint can miss the point when general progress makes a filtered skill easier to recover.

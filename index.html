This week’s feature · underdog direction

More examples—or more kinds of examples?

A recent tool-use study suggests that, when the goal is handling unfamiliar situations, what training examples cover may matter more than how many you collect.

“Add more training data” sounds like a safe bet. But if the new examples repeat the same patterns, a model may get better at familiar tasks without becoming much more reliable when the tools or combinations change. For general-purpose AI, that gap between familiar performance and out-of-distribution (OOD) performance is a real scaling problem.

The underdog result: variety beats raw volume in one controlled setting

The ICML 2026 paper DIVE: Scaling Diversity in Agentic Task Synthesis for Generalizable Tool Use studies synthetic training tasks for tool-using language models. The authors trace brittle generalization to a lack of diversity across tools, tool combinations, and patterns of tool use. Their approach runs a range of real tools first, then derives training tasks from the resulting execution traces so examples remain grounded and verifiable.

The scale is substantial: 48,000 trajectories across 374 tools and five domains, covering 46,398 unique toolsets. In experiments with Qwen3-8B and nine OOD benchmarks, the DIVE training recipe improved the average score by 22 points and outperformed the strongest 8B baseline by 68 percent. In a fixed-budget comparison, the authors report that “diversity scaling consistently outperforms quantity scaling”, even with four times less data.

That does not mean example count is irrelevant. The result is about a specific model, synthetic tool-use training, and OOD generalization. The stronger lesson is that “more” has dimensions: more examples, more tool coverage, more combinations, and more varied interaction patterns. If you only scale the first, you can spend more compute on variations of the same experience.

Where the hidden cost moves

Diversity is not free. Creating examples across many tools means keeping each task executable and checking that its expected result is valid. DIVE grounds tasks in traces to address that challenge; as a practical inference, teams adopting this direction should budget for tool maintenance, verification, and evaluation—not only generation. The cheap-looking dataset can be expensive if no one can tell whether its examples are valid or representative.

A better comparison for your next run

Hold the model, token budget, and training recipe steady. Then compare two additions: more examples from the current distribution, and a smaller set that expands the structural coverage of tools, domains, or task paths. Evaluate both on held-out combinations the training data never used. If the aim is robustness under change, the OOD result should decide which dataset earned its cost.

Bulleted breakdown

  • Name the target. “Better performance” could mean familiar accuracy or generalization to new tools and task combinations.
  • Count the kinds of experience. Track domains, toolsets, and interaction patterns alongside total examples.
  • Control the budget. Compare quantity and diversity with similar compute and training conditions.
  • Validate every generated task. Measure the effort needed to keep examples executable and their outcomes checkable.
  • Use held-out shifts. Test novel combinations, not just another random split from familiar tasks.

The takeaway: Data scaling is not one dial. This study’s result suggests that for tool-use generalization, adding different kinds of experience can beat adding more of the same—but that finding still needs validation on your model, task, and budget.

01 / AFTER REVIEW

More data still wins in some domains

An ICML 2026 study of global weather models reports that a tenfold dataset increase reduced Aurora’s validation loss by up to 3.2 times. Under fixed compute, its analysis favored allocating more resources to training data over model size.

Why this matters: The DIVE result is not a universal “less data is better” rule; the payoff depends on domain, objective, and data mix.

Practical direction · inference

Count coverage and verification cost

For a controlled pilot, compare a larger familiar dataset with a more varied dataset at similar compute. Track coverage, valid-task rate, and held-out generalization so additional diversity is judged by useful outcomes rather than variety on paper alone.

Why this matters: Broader data only pays off when the examples are valid and improve the behavior you need.

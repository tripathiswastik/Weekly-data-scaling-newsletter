Does more data always make a better model?

One question, every day: when does scaling training data improve deep learning—and when do quality, repetition, compute, or infrastructure change the answer?

Today’s feature · wrong direction to better targeting

A smaller fact set helped a small model remember more

A 2026 ICML study finds that adding every fact can crowd a model’s limited memory. Targeted data selection improved fact recall—but the selection process itself carried a compute bill.

When a model misses a fact, the conventional response is often to add more training data or grow the model. But if the model has limited capacity and the training set contains many facts with a long-tailed frequency distribution, simply showing it everything can make factual recall worse. A new study tests a different lever: decide which facts get training attention.

Too many facts can overfill the model’s memory budget

In Cram Less to Fit More: Training Data Pruning Improves Memorization of Facts, Jiayuan Ye, Vitaly Feldman and Kunal Talwar formalize a capacity limit for fact accuracy. Their controlled experiments find that once the information in the facts exceeds that capacity, standard training can fall short of the best achievable recall. A skewed frequency distribution—where a small set of facts dominates and many appear rarely—makes the gap worse.

The proposed selection methods use training loss as a proxy for how fact information is distributed. They aim to keep the number of facts within the model’s capacity and reduce frequency imbalance. On semi-synthetic, high-entropy fact datasets, the approach raises accuracy to the paper’s theoretical capacity limit. In annotated Wikipedia pretraining, a 110-million-parameter GPT-2 Small model selected on only a fraction of the factual material memorized 1.3 times as many entity facts as standard training; on that fact metric, it matched a 1.3-billion-parameter model trained on the full dataset.

The result is specific to factual memorization, not a claim that a smaller model is now better at every language task. The authors also measured knowledge-focused MMLU and general natural-language understanding. The knowledge subset improved more modestly and noisily; general NLU stayed roughly stable at most selection ratios, but dropped at the most aggressive 10% setting. The task target changes the best selection ratio: the paper reports about 20% for fact accuracy and 50% for knowledge-MMLU.

The hidden cost: selection is not free

There is a trade-off behind the headline. To select useful examples online, the method may need extra forward passes to gather enough candidates for each training batch. The paper says this increases training FLOPs per iteration. Its results focus on sufficient-training regimes and compare against longer full-data training; it leaves a strict, bounded-FLOP comparison as an open problem. The authors also grid-search the selection ratio, which adds tuning work.

Evidence: In the reported Wikipedia experiment, data selection boosted fact accuracy and the knowledge-focused task while generally preserving NLU. The paper is in the 2026 Proceedings of Machine Learning Research and reports three runs for the 110M model’s fact-accuracy result.

Inference: If the goal is reliable recall of a defined fact set, selective training may be worth testing before buying a larger model or collecting still more text. But compare end-to-end compute, selection overhead and tuning effort—not just the number of retained facts.

Limits: The striking 1.3× result measures entity-fact memorization on an annotated Wikipedia setup. It does not prove improvements in open-ended reasoning, broad language quality or every domain. Fact selection also depends on annotated fact boundaries in the real-corpus experiment.

Bulleted breakdown

  • Capacity matters. If a small model must encode too many facts, standard training may not reach its best possible fact accuracy.
  • Frequency skew adds pressure. Common facts can crowd out rare but important facts.
  • Targeted selection helped on a factual metric. The 110M model retained 1.3× more entity facts than standard training in the reported setup.
  • General performance was not the same outcome. Knowledge-MMLU gains were noisier, and very aggressive selection hurt NLU.
  • Count selection compute too. Extra forward passes and choosing a selection ratio affect the real cost.

The takeaway: More data is not automatically better when a model must encode more facts than it can reliably retain. Curating for the target can help—but evaluate the target, protect general capability and include selection overhead in the budget.

Why this matters: The best data budget depends on what the model must learn and how expensive it is to identify the useful examples.

01 / WRONG DIRECTION

“Keep every fact” can work against small-model recall

The paper’s capacity analysis and long-tail experiments suggest that more factual content can overload a model when its memory budget is limited.

Why this matters: Increasing corpus size without checking fact coverage may spend training on information the model cannot retain reliably.

02 / HIDDEN COST

Pruning saved facts, but selection added forward passes

The authors note that candidate selection increases training FLOPs per iteration, and their experiments tune the retained fraction rather than assuming one setting works for every goal.

Why this matters: Fewer training examples do not guarantee a cheaper training run once data-selection compute is counted.

After review · check the metric

Fact recall improved more clearly than broad task scores

The study reports stronger gains on fact accuracy, smaller and noisier gains on knowledge-focused MMLU, and mostly stable NLU except under the most aggressive selection. Follow-up evaluations should keep these outcomes separate.

Why this matters: A data recipe can improve the objective it targets without improving every capability users care about.

Today’s feature · underdog perspective

When the domain is small, repetition can be a scaling strategy

A data-constrained study finds that scarce specialist or low-resource-language corpora can still contribute at scale—if they are mixed with general data and repeated deliberately.

Many training plans quietly assume that a model can always be fed more distinct examples. That assumption breaks for low-resource languages, specialized fields, and other domains where the useful corpus is finite. Teams then face an apparent choice: underrepresent the target domain with generic data, or repeat a small target set until the model overfits.

More than 2,000 training runs test the trade-off

In Scaling Laws for Mixture Pretraining Under Data Constraints, Anastasiia Sedova, Skyler Seto, Natalie Schluter, and Pierre Ablin study mixtures of scarce target data and abundant generic data. Their experiments span more than 2,000 language-model training runs, multiple model and target-dataset sizes, and multilingual, domain-specific, and quality-filtered data.

The central finding challenges the simple rule “never repeat data.” In the studied mixtures, repetition is a major driver of target-domain performance, and mixed training tolerates more reuse than training on a single source. The authors report that scarce target corpora can be reused 15–20 times, but the best repetition count changes with corpus size, compute budget, and model scale. It is not a universal setting to copy into every recipe.

Why can repeating examples help? Generic data supplies breadth, while repeated target examples increase exposure to the specific language or domain the model must handle. The paper models both effects with a repetition-aware mixture scaling law: repeated target tokens provide diminishing value, while the generic component has a regularizing role. The goal is to choose a mixture based on the target corpus and budget instead of treating every repeated pass as either waste or free extra data.

From “find more data” to “budget the exposure”

The practical change is to measure effective target exposure, not just the raw size of the corpus. Record how often target examples recur, what proportion of training tokens comes from the generic pool, and how the model performs on held-out target examples. Sweep mixture ratios and repetition under a fixed compute budget; include a no-repeat or low-repeat baseline so the team can see when added passes stop helping.

Evidence boundary: The authors tested particular training runs, data mixtures, and model scales. The 15–20 reuse range is their reported result across the studied settings, not a general promise that repetition will improve every model, or that repeated examples equal new independent information.

Inference: For teams blocked on acquiring more specialist data, it may be more useful to first optimize how existing examples are mixed and revisited. That is a lower-cost experiment than launching a new collection effort, but only target-specific held-out evaluation can show whether it worked.

Bulleted breakdown

  • Measure scarcity. Track distinct target examples separately from total tokens seen.
  • Mix for purpose. Combine scarce target data with general data instead of assuming either source should dominate.
  • Count repeats. Log target-data exposure and test several repetition levels under matched compute.
  • Hold out target examples. Check specialist or low-resource performance, plus general capability, after every recipe change.
  • Treat 15–20 as evidence from this study. The optimum depends on data size, budget, and model scale.

The takeaway: When new independent data is scarce, scaling can mean allocating the examples you have more deliberately. Repetition may help inside a mixed-data recipe, but the correct amount is an empirical choice, not a universal constant.

After review · what to validate

Count unique examples and repeat exposures separately

A repeated token is not a new independent observation. When comparing a mixed recipe with a larger corpus, track both unique target examples and total target exposures, then score against held-out target data.

Why this matters: The distinction shows whether a gain came from useful domain exposure or from counting passes as though they were new data.

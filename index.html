Today’s feature · wrong direction to better direction

More rows can make the wrong future look predictable

A large real-world tabular study finds that adding training data often helps—but under natural distribution shifts, in-distribution and out-of-distribution results can part company at scale.

“More data improves performance” sounds like a universal rule because many experiments draw train and test examples from the same distribution. In production, that assumption often fails: customer behavior changes, sensors drift, fraud patterns adapt, or the population shifts. A larger training set can describe the past in greater detail without becoming a better guide to the next population.

Eight datasets, up to roughly ten million examples

In On Data Scaling in Tabular ML: More Data, Better Generalization?, Ivan Rubachev, Nikita Lazarev, and Artem Babenko examine eight real-world tabular datasets, retaining natural shifts and using random splits as a control. They scale training sets to around ten million examples and study gradient-boosted trees, multilayer perceptrons (MLPs), and tabular foundation models.

The broad result is not “more data hurts.” For many tasks and models, more examples improve test performance, sometimes through the largest sizes studied. But the paper’s more important finding concerns what “test performance” means. At smaller training sizes, in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) results mostly move together. At larger sizes, they can diverge: extra data may continue improving ID performance while OOD performance stalls or worsens, especially when MLPs are selected using ID validation data.

The authors connect this divergence to model selection. As the training set grows, choices such as architecture width, hyperparameters, and early stopping matter more for OOD generalization. If validation data resembles the training distribution but not the production target, selecting the model that wins on that validation set can reward the wrong behavior. In their experiments, validation more representative of evaluation conditions helps MLP-based models maintain or improve OOD performance as training data scales.

A validation split is part of the scaling recipe

This is evidence from tabular tasks with natural shifts, not proof that every dataset behaves this way. The paper also finds that newer tabular foundation models benefit more consistently from larger in-context examples, although that does not eliminate every OOD decline in the tested settings. Model family and how validation is used both change the outcome.

The practical correction is to stop treating a random split as a universal proxy for tomorrow. For time-dependent data, validate on later periods. For geographic or customer shifts, hold out regions or cohorts that resemble deployment. Track ID and OOD metrics separately as training data grows, and compare models at matched evaluation conditions.

Inference: If adding examples improves the familiar benchmark but not the deployment-relevant holdout, the next investment may be better target-aligned evaluation, data coverage, or model selection—not simply another expansion of the training corpus. The study does not prescribe one universal validation design; the right split depends on the shift the system must survive.

Bulleted breakdown

  • Keep the control. Compare random-split results with realistic time, geography, or cohort shifts.
  • Scale the evaluation too. Monitor ID and OOD curves at each training-set size instead of reporting one blended score.
  • Match validation to deployment. Test whether target-representative validation changes which model wins.
  • Separate evidence from inference. Divergence was observed in the studied tasks; applying this diagnostic elsewhere is a practical recommendation, not a universal result.

The takeaway: More examples often help, but “better” depends on where performance is measured. Under distribution shift, scaling the training set without aligning model selection and validation to deployment can improve the score you see while weakening the generalization you need.

After review · evaluation lesson

Plot two curves, not one

As training data grows, report performance on both an ID test set and a deployment-relevant OOD set. The study finds these can diverge at larger scales; target-representative validation helped MLP selection in the examined tasks.

Why this matters: Separate curves reveal whether new data improves the target you care about or mainly the distribution you already know.

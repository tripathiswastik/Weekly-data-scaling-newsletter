Today’s feature · from one-size-fits-all to task fit

Same number of words. Different model skills.

A paired study of small language models finds no universal “best” corpus: two equal-size text sources produced different strengths on different benchmarks.

“More data” is easy to count; “better data” is harder to define. A corpus can teach some patterns especially well while providing little practice for others. That makes one aggregate score a risky guide to scaling: it can hide a model that improved on one task and slipped on another.

A controlled comparison of old and new text

In Different Corpora, Different Skills? A Paired Study of Small Language Models, Edoardo Vaira, Georgios Chavales and Pierre Nugues compare the 1911 Encyclopaedia Britannica with the heterogeneous BabyLM 2026 strict corpus. They train at matched 10-million- and 37-million-word budgets, using the same model families, training recipes and evaluation procedures. The study includes teachers, 58-million-parameter Baby-Llama students trained with knowledge distillation, and student models without distillation.

Across five benchmarks, the outcome is not one corpus winning everywhere. BabyLM is consistently stronger on linguistic acceptability tests; Britannica is often stronger on Entity Tracking, a synthetic task that checks whether a model follows changing facts about entities through a text. The difference appears for teacher and student models and at both word budgets. In the clearest replicated student comparison, the corpus choice shifts the model’s relative skill profile.

The team also runs targeted controls. The central contrast persists when they match training exposure and test with a longer diagnostic context. Directly ensembling the teachers does not reproduce the Britannica student’s Entity Tracking behavior. Still, the size of that Entity Tracking advantage varies with training dynamics, so the paper does not establish a clean, universal rule about encyclopedic text.

The hidden budget: what “same size” means

The comparison matches word counts and training procedure, but the authors explicitly note that each corpus learns its own vocabulary. As a result, the two conditions do not have equal token counts or identical vocabularies. That matters when interpreting “same data budget”: equal words are a fair controlled choice for this question, but they are not the only resource measure. Tokenization can change how much sequence the model processes and which units it sees.

Evidence: For this corpus pair and small-model setup, the paper reports different relative benchmark strengths rather than a single overall quality ranking. The work appears in the Proceedings of the Second BabyLM Workshop; the arXiv version includes the same study and limitations.

Inference: If a team adds more of one source, the score that matters should be the target task and the evaluation set—not a convenient average alone. A useful scaling experiment records words, tokens, vocabulary, compute and task-by-task results. Otherwise, the data budget may grow while the intended skill barely moves.

Limits: This is a controlled study of two corpora and 58M student models, not a survey of all pretraining sources or frontier systems. Entity Tracking is synthetic, and even the authors note training-dynamics sensitivity. The result is a reason to test corpus fit, not proof that either historical or child-directed text is generally superior.

Bulleted breakdown

  • Same word budget, different sources. The study compares Britannica and BabyLM at 10M and 37M words under matched training procedures.
  • Strengths split by task. BabyLM did better on linguistic acceptability; Britannica often did better on synthetic entity tracking.
  • No single quality ladder emerged. The winner depended on which capability was measured.
  • Words are not tokens. Corpus-specific vocabularies mean equal word budgets did not equalize token counts.
  • Scale against the goal. Pair resource accounting with several held-out benchmarks tied to the intended use.

The takeaway: A larger corpus can improve measured performance, but corpus content helps determine which performance. “Scale it” is incomplete advice until the team defines the task, counts the budget carefully and checks more than one outcome.

More signals from today’s research

01 / HIDDEN ASSUMPTION

Equal words did not mean equal tokens

The paired study holds source-word budgets and procedures steady, but corpus-specific tokenizers produce different vocabularies and token counts.

Why this matters: Report both words and tokens so “same-size dataset” does not conceal a different training exposure.

02 / WRONG DIRECTION

Replace “best corpus” with “best fit for this task”

BabyLM and Britannica trade places across linguistic acceptability and Entity Tracking rather than forming one universal quality ranking.

Why this matters: A score can rise on average while a capability the product needs remains weak.

After review · next validation

Repeat the comparison on your own target skills

The result supports multi-benchmark checks, but it covers two sources and small student models. Follow-up work should vary corpus pairs, tokenize under matched token budgets as well as word budgets, and repeat seeds on the metrics that matter in deployment.

Why this matters: The follow-up separates real corpus effects from choices about vocabulary, exposure and a narrow benchmark.
